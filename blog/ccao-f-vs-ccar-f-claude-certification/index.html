CCAO-F vs CCAR-F: which Claude certification should you take?

By CCA Practice Platforms · August 27, 2026

Anthropic's certification programme publishes more than one Foundations exam, and the codes sit one character apart. CCAO-F is the Claude Certified Associate – Foundations exam. CCAR-F is the Claude Certified Architect – Foundations exam, the one you will also see written informally as CCA-F — we covered why that exam answers to two different codes separately.

They are not two tiers of one test. They are aimed at different people doing different work, and the published exam guides say so directly. This article sets the two side by side using only what those guides state, so you can work out which one you are actually preparing for.

The short answer

If you use Claude as a productivity tool — drafting, analysis, research, process work — the Associate exam is the one written for you. If you build with Claude — agentic systems, tool integrations, production pipelines — the Architect exam is. Both guides draw that line, and they draw it in the same place.

What each credential says it validates

The Claude Certified Associate – Foundations guide opens (Section 1) by describing a credential that validates applying Claude to complete business and productivity tasks with minimal guidance: using built-in platform features and tools to streamline workflows, identifying opportunities to improve processes, selecting approaches that balance quality against efficiency and cost, and recognising limitations well enough to escalate more complex or technical work.

The Claude Certified Architect – Foundations guide describes something different in its own Section 1: foundational knowledge across Claude Code, the Claude Agent SDK, the Claude API and the Model Context Protocol, with questions grounded in production contexts such as customer-support agents, multi-agent research pipelines and CI/CD integrations. Its Section 2 sets out an ideal candidate who is a solution architect, typically with six or more months of practical experience building against those technologies.

The audience distinction is the whole story

Section 3 of the Associate guide is unusually direct about who the exam is not for. It names software developers who build against APIs or design agentic systems, alongside specialists in machine learning, software engineering and advanced AI system design, and states that candidates are not expected to design enterprise-scale AI architectures or integrations. Then it says where that work belongs: “that scope belongs to the Claude Architect and Claude Developer credentials”, to which Associates escalate more complex work.

That is the clearest signal either document gives, and it comes from the Associate guide itself rather than from us. The Associate exam is written for professionals in operations, marketing, project management, education, communications and general knowledge work — both internal staff who maintain AI-enabled workflows and external consultants who support implementation and process redesign. Section 3 places them between casual prompt users and technical AI practitioners, expecting limited to moderate technical expertise.

So if you are writing the integration rather than using it, the Associate guide is pointing you at the Architect exam. Our longer piece on which roles the Architect certification actually fits works through that question from the other direction.

Where the two exams are identical

This is the part that surprises people. Almost every mechanical detail matches.

Item formatMultiple-choice and multiple-response; each item states how many responses to selectMultiple-choice and multiple-response; each item states how many responses to select
Passing scoreScaled score of 720 on a scale of 100–1,000Scaled score of 720 on a scale of 100–1,000
DeliveryProctored: online and/or test centreProctored: online and/or test centre

Exam fee$99 USD$125 USD

Sixty items, two hours, and the same scaled score of 720 on a 100–1,000 range. Both guides state the item format in identical words, and neither guide says what proportion of items takes each form — a point worth holding onto, because it is easy to assume otherwise. We wrote about what multiple-response actually means for how you answer when the Architect guide's wording raised the same question.

Both exams also report results the same way: a pass or fail with a scaled score, plus percent-correct by content domain, with the section-level percentages explicitly not used to determine the outcome. Both are criterion-referenced assessments, so you are measured against a fixed performance standard rather than against other candidates.

The policies match as well. Registration and scheduling for both run through the Anthropic Partner Academy and Pearson VUE. Both credentials renew through a free, non-proctored assessment on the Partner Academy, and a lapsed credential means retaking the full exam at the full fee. Both set the same waiting periods after a failed attempt — fourteen days after the first, thirty after the second, ninety after the third — with a maximum of four attempts in a rolling twelve-month period and the fee payable on each one. Both guides add that these limits apply per exam, so not passing one does not stop you registering for the other.

Where they genuinely differ

The fee

The Associate exam is $99 USD, from Section 5 of its guide. The Architect exam is $125 USD, from Section 3 of its own. That is the only figure in the two at-a-glance tables that differs.

The blueprint

The Associate exam draws from seven content domains; the Architect exam from five. The subject matter barely overlaps, which is the audience distinction expressed as a syllabus.

The Associate blueprint (Section 6) weights its seven domains like this:

CCAO-F content domainWeight
Prompting and Task Execution14%
Output Evaluation and Validation21%
Product and Model Selection12%
Workflow Integration and Solution Design16%
Configuration and Knowledge Management12%
Governance, Risk, and Responsible Use15%
Troubleshooting and Optimization10%

The heaviest single domain is Output Evaluation and Validation at 21% — judging whether what Claude produced is accurate and complete, identifying hallucinations and bias, and determining when human review is required. Add Governance, Risk, and Responsible Use at 15% and more than a third of the Associate exam turns on judgement about output and appropriate use rather than on construction.

The Architect blueprint (Section 4) is five domains, and they are engineering subjects:

CCAR-F content domainWeight
Agentic Architecture & Orchestration27%
Tool Design & MCP Integration18%
Claude Code Configuration & Workflows20%
Prompt Engineering & Structured Output20%
Context Management & Reliability15%

Agentic Architecture & Orchestration alone is 27%, covering agentic loops, coordinator-subagent patterns and subagent context passing. No Associate domain corresponds to it. We break down the five Architect domains and what each one weighs in a separate post.

Prompting appears on both blueprints, and it is the one place they touch. Even there the framing differs: the Associate guide's Domain 1 is about creating effective prompts, decomposing tasks and iterating to improve output quality, while the Architect guide's Prompt Engineering & Structured Output domain runs to JSON schemas, structured output via tool use, and extraction patterns.

Exam structure

The Architect guide specifies its structure in Section 3 and expands on it in Section 5: four scenarios drawn from a bank of six, presented at random, each framing a set of questions around a production context such as a customer-support resolution agent or a CI/CD integration. The Associate guide's at-a-glance table carries no equivalent exam-structure row.

How to choose

A few questions that follow directly from the guides:

  • Do you write code that calls Claude? If yes, the Architect exam is the one aimed at you. If no, the Associate guide has explicitly told you its exam is not.
  • Is your day mostly building or mostly using? Configuring a Claude Project with instructions and knowledge sources is Associate territory. Designing a coordinator-subagent system is Architect territory.
  • Do you have hands-on time with the Agent SDK, Claude Code or MCP? The Architect guide's Section 2 assumes roughly six months or more of it. The Associate guide's Section 4 states there are no mandatory prerequisites or required courses, and that no software-development or API experience is needed.
  • Are you weighing cost against effort? The fee gap is $26, which is small next to the preparation each demands. Our assessment of whether the Architect certification is worth taking covers that trade-off, and how demanding the Architect exam actually is covers the preparation side.

Nothing in either guide ranks one credential above the other, and neither describes one as a prerequisite for the other. They describe different scopes of work. The Associate guide's own escalation language, in which Associates hand more complex or technical work to Architects and Developers, describes a division of labour rather than a ladder.
